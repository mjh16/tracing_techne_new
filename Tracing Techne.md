The 2018 publication of *Composition, Rhetoric, and Disciplinarity* marks a recently renewed attempt to identify and qualify the contemporary social moment in Rhetoric and Composition (Malenczyk et al.). Part of the commonality that binds Rhetoric and Composition together in our contemporary moment, as Malenczyk et al. describe it, is a devotion to pedagogy and an orientation toward service defined by inquiry into manifestations of language in this thing we call writing that make it look different from other disciplines (2018, pp. 4-5). In that publication, what Malenczyk et al call a “disciplinary” turn  invites a critical examination of the field’s history. As a collection, *Composition, Rhetoric, and Disciplinarity* draws attention to the assumptions and work of the field of Rhetoric and Composition and, in doing so, asks us to re-examine the foundations of what it means to study writing.

## Why *techne,* why now?

*Techne* is an important assumptions for writing, rhetoric, and the teaching of writing given its basis in the art and craft of rhetoric. There is a need for considering that there is something that is teachable, learnable, and masterable with rhetoric as a disciplinary concern. A *techne,* a skill or ability, is needed in order to act rhetorically. Additionally, there is a sense of what might be necessary to consider various levels of attainment or mastery, but not a clear sense of how one is considered at those different levels without some clear guidance. *Techne* is assumed and theorized as necessary for a rhetor to exist and to make change.  However, as Kelly Pender (2011), Byron Hawk (2007), and Jacqueline Rhodes and Jonathon Alexander (2015) continue to demonstrate, *techne* is a slippery concept. Some of the difficulty in considering what *techne* is and what its role in current theory and pedagogy is the multiplicity of definitions, traditions, and assumptions. Additionally, there are disagreements on the value and role of *techne* itself that derive from these assumptions. 

Another issue, as most succinctly, presented by Ulmer (1994), is that *techne* often becomes conflated with invention. It’s more than just invention, a where/how ideas and discourse emerge, but the interwoven notions of invention, creativity, design, production, and teaching where *techne* resides. *Techne* is a valuable concept for understanding the epistemologies of the discipline of Rhetoric and Composition because of its ability to connect invention, language, and people “with the process of making” (Pender, 2011, p. 4) and has implications for how we understand where writing comes from and how we make epistemological claims about writing. Much as Janice M. Lauer point to invention being scattered to a number of places in the 1990s (2002) so to does *techne*. 	Part of the ambiguity and complexity of *techne*, I would argue, is its positioning between invention and generous conceptions of creativity and technology. Furthermore, *techne* also intersects with issues of power, privilege, literacy, and access, and has deep implications for who is able to speak/write, but also if/how others might listen and be moved. Further, notions of *techne* are further complicated by the historiography of the concept. How history is made is a rhetorical process, and has deep implications for how disciplines develop (Baliff,  2013; Graban & Sullivan, 2018). Historiography has influenced disciplinary assumptions, histories, theories, practices, and pedagogies. As important as history is, historiography is equally as important, especially, I would argue, for disciplines.

*Techne* seems especially important for the development of the discipline of Rhetoric and Composition in the 1990s - and for discussions and arguments of disciplinarity for Rhetoric and Composition then and now—so it needs to be contended with. In contending with the historiography of *techne* in/from the 1990s, this study is guided by two overarching questions:

* Where is *techne* in 1990s scholarship?
* What are the implications of the uptake of *techne* in the 1990s?

However, there is little direct theorization in the 1990s of *techne*. When starting this project, I searched for “techne” in *CompPile*, a common disciplinary repository and search tool, bounded to the years 1990-1999, and came up with 9 results including two doctoral thesis and an article in German. Perhaps *techne* is a casualty of the post-process turn in the field. Maybe as issues of process, and especially cognition were turned aside for important cultural and social critiques, issues of *techne* were subsumed in other conversation. Maybe they were overlooked entirely. However, *techne* does get picked up in the early 2000s and onward with work by Atwill, Lauer, and Sirc with in relationship to theories of invention and how they figure into rhetorical pedagogy with digital technology.

## Techne and invention

### *Techne*

*Techne* is the core concept of this project. Beginning with Pender’s five composite definitions, *techne* is often perceived as

1. a “how-to” guide or handbook
2. a rational ability to effect a useful result
3. a means of inventing new social possibilities
4. a means of producing resources
5. a non-instrumental mode of bringing forth (2011, p. 16)

These five composite definitions arise within different historical moments, and each represent “epistemological and axiological positions” (Pender, 2011, p. 16) through which the concept is taken up and expressed. Each of these five composite definitions helps to “explain how *techne* can be defined in such different, sometimes contradictory ways \[…\] exist\[ing\] on a continuum” (Pender, 2011, p. 38) with each of these positions retaining a certain amount of staying power. These five composite definition of *techne* serve as the basis for the inquiry into the appearances and impressions of *techne* within the scholarship of the 1990s, and help to identify places within the data analysis where *techne* might be found. Not only does each composite definition continue to influence each progressive uptake of *techne*, but they also persist within practice and within the disciplinary discourse of rhetoric and composition (Pender, 2011, p. 38). This critical view of invention as *techne*, as a practiced and embodied art, is one that echoes Young’s earlier explorations into invention, and lays the groundwork for a new historiography that focuses on the in-betweenness that *techne* can inhabit. 

### Invention

Invention, as a basis for rhetorical action, exists at the productive tension between a specific teachable practice “scattered across an array of activities, modes, and spatiotemporal openings” which potentially opens it to “the totality of rhetoric theory and practice” (Simonson, 2014, p. 300). The canon of invention has been theorized as “the discovery of the content of a discourse” (Delagrange, 2011, p. 109), and one that concerned with arts that can inform practice across situations (Atwill, 2002, p. xii). Theorizing invention as teachable art which can be used in different situations, as Lauer summarizes, lies within the interaction between *episteme* and *techne* (2002, p. 11). This utilitarian and instrumental notion of invention is held in classical rhetorical theory, and throughout much theorizing of the canon, with Young seeing invention as the art and craft of rhetoric, as best produced and applied through the use and generation of tagmemic and heuristic approaches (Young, 1980, p. 348).

However, this instrumental approach does not appear to be compatible with the subjectivities that postmodernism brings to discourse. John Mucklebauer further complicates notions of invention through a reconceptualization of *doxa* that is “consistently figured as the material on which (and through which) \[rhetoricians\] work” (2008, p. 150) which echoes the shifting landscapes of *techne* that Pender traces. Both Pender and Mucklebauer explore invention through the postmodern dual motion of opening up and closing down, the re-invention of the known in the guise of the unknown, arriving at different interventions—re-theorized *techne* and *doxa* respectively—and pushing on the assumptions of how rhetoric comes to be in the world. Thomas Rickert further complicates this postmodern conception of invention through an examination of how rhetoric both relies upon material things, and yet excludes them its theorization, complicating issues of subjectivity and agency (2013, p. 192). This reliance upon a material world from which to make rhetoric, and yet the hiding of it within the theorization of rhetoric obscures and refigure invention, and thereby an epistemology of rhetoric. By bringing issues of materiality into conversation with the social-epistemic nature of rhetoric, considering the interwoveness of *techne* must account for immense and partial perspectives of language and matter when considering acts of invention.

In order to bridge these tensions and dispersals of invention, Peter Simonson offers the term “*inventional media*, to refer to the habits, artistic materials, and communicative modes through which rhetorical generation occurs” (2014, p. 300). Certainly, the epistemological gains of the social turn, and the ability of critical perspectives to interpret and intervene in the world are powerful, but individual imaginative acts of bringing forth have seemingly been lost in the shift towards invention emerging out of social-cultural contexts that “both enable and constrain invention, providing scenes and resources through which it takes shape” (Simonson, 2014, p. 314). 

Invention is an important concept for approaching history in rhetoric and composition given its role in making epistemological claims about writing. The late Janice Lauer traces an “inventional turn” that, during the 1990s, dispersed through many sites and perspectives (2004, pp. 96-116), away from the efforts to categorize, theorize, and test elaborated theories of process and towards the knowledges made and expressed through invention (p. 118). Invention has often been obscured by discussions of cultural critique and postmodern theory that shifted attention in the 1990s towards issues of social construction, ideology, and the origins of language. As discussed by both Kelly Pender and Byron Hawk, Richard Young’s work in the 1970s and 1980s on process theorized the labor of rhetoric and composition to be the work of invention, broadly understood, and saw *techne* as a means of connecting invention explicitly to writing (Hawk, 2007, p. 27; Pender, 2011, p. 39). Given these roots between invention and *techne* historically, this research is interested in these specific intersections.

### Techne and Invention

*Techne* is a valuable concept for understanding the epistemologies of discipline of Rhetoric and Composition because of its ability to connect invention, language, and people “with the process of making” (Pender, 2011, p. 4) and has implications for how we understand where writing comes from and how we make epistemological claims about writing. *Techne* provides a framework for understanding writing as a rhetorical practice that can be studied, taught, and deployed with social contexts. *Techne* is often theorized as being concerned with invention, especially in moving from the production of language in composition to its social and rhetorical functioning in the world (Hawk, 2007, p. 88; Pender, 2011, p. 6). Yet, invention is rarely discussed with *techne*, especially in connecting to the technological and material aspects of composing, leaving invention scattered and diffused within disciplinary histories. Kelly Pender argues that, when issues of *techne* are found within other conversations, they are often obscured in discussions of invention, imagination, creativity, or creation (2011, p. 8). Additionally, as the late Janice Lauer has argued, the history of invention was particularly problematic in the 1990s as it had “migrated, entered, settled, and shaped many other areas of theory and practice in rhetoric and composition” (2002, p. 2), and this diffusion, along with the absence of *techne* from scholarly conversations about invention makes *techne* challenging to trace. Furthermore, *techne* is often obscured in the discipline’s most formative decade: the social turn of the 1980s, where the focus of the discipline turned from issues of process to issues of the social construction of knowledge and epistemologies of writing, and the introduction of computerized and networked technologies in the 1990s, where the field’s histories became more aligned with critical examination of pedagogy and technology. 

By recouping *techne* as an embodied digital feminist practice, Susan Delagrange bridges invention in new media texts through four characteristics of *techne*: that “it is heuristic, situated, strategically mobile, and ethical” (2011, p. 16). In working to theorize a feminist digital rhetoric, she illustrates how the seeming contradictions of *techne* as a feminist practice that pairs invention with intervention alongside issues of production in multimedia and hypermedia spaces. This theorization serves as a place that engages both composer and audience as co-navigators of texts and their potentials seeing *techne* which “require reflection, cognitive engagement, and a willingness to defer closure as ideas and connections percolate” (Delagrange, 2011, p. 129). She brings together a feminist invention with *techne*, digital rhetoric, composition, and literacy in an embodied exploration of hypermediated writing. This place of radically productive uncertainty position *techne* not just as a craft and theory of production, but as an avenue by which writing may come to make meaning. *Techne* offers a departure from mechanistic theories of invention such as Young’s which focus on the ability to craft and find places from which to create discourse and turn us towards notions of finding ways to experience and re-experience language. 

Additionally, as Pender argues, invention and *techne* have been closely aligned within rhetoric and composition scholarship, shaping theory, practice, and pedagogies within the discipline while illuminating that *techne* is oftentimes under-theorized and under-utilized in that same scholarship (Pender, 2011, p. 12). The in-betweenness that *techne* inhabits highlights explicit convergences of imagination and rhetorical practice inherent with acts of creation which can be understood not as serendipity experienced by a certain few, but as a practice that can be learned, revised, recalled, and utilized (Pender, 2011, p. 141). Additionally, as both Pender and Lauer have indicated, tensions between theories of invention and *techne* have left material traces within the scholarship and practices of rhetoric and composition. 

## Historiography

### Towards a distributed history - tracing impressions and data curation

Disciplinary historiography is often a result of citations and citational politics (Brooke, 2012). These are also influenced by large events, notable articles, certain books, the moment of a particular address that drive attention in some particular ways and away from others. In this process, as Brooke (2012) describes, and Mueller (2012) illustrates, certain texts and ideas are given greater weight in disciplinary history, and form an implicit core of the value of a discipline. In Rhetoric and Composition broadly, we can see particular attention to process, post-process, genre, rhetorical situation, critical pedagogy, discplinarity, and reflection as some of the major touchstones of that history. These can often be tied around particular figures, texts, and moments, with identifiable actors and texts. People publish, ideas become sticky, moments happen, and history is made. These moments are often named after-the-fact where the stickiness can be seen.

This historiographic process highlights certain texts, authors, and ideas as important, cited, and elevated while leaving many others (that are important in their own way) to the side. However, these texts that have been published, read, and circulated all contribute to disciplinary knowledge and shaped the reading of what are now elevated texts at the time, and continue to influence their understanding and assumptions in new contexts.

By examining the discipline’s perspectives on *techne,* we might challenge the field’s “monolithic sense of its history” (Skinnell, 2015, p. 120) through challenging epistemologies and demanding a (responsible) response, unsettling comfortable notions, and critiquing core assumptions (Ballif, 2013b, p. 141). The critical impulses are vital, but as Skinnell points out, recovery and broadening of rhetorical history has trended towards gazes of expansion and inclusion and have tended towards reification of core assumptions of the field. Rhetorical historiography invites opening up of the past and its formation of the present to examination to direct the attention towards the formation of that history and through peering into the spaces in-between *techne* and the material left behind in the formation of that history. A critical perspective on historiography reminds us that history is itself rhetorical, and its uptake and circulation not only shape how we think about *techne*, but also the actions that *techne* allows.

Carole Blair argues in her 1992 article that “histories of rhetorical theory are not neutral or objective reconstructions of facts,” but are rhetorical in their construction (Blair, 1992, p. 417), and that the construction of histories require critical consideration. A critical study of rhetorical history, or rhetorical historiography, has “serious entailments for how we understand rhetoric’s theoretical past” (Blair, 1992, p. 405), and historiographic choices also shape how we position our place in relationship to rhetorical history (Blair, 1992, p. 417). Rhetorical historiography also offers “a rigorous examination of the values historians advance, knowingly or not” (Skinnell, 2015, p. 113) that inform current theory and practice. Historical assumptions and values surrounding the role of *techne* being particularly vexed because of a lack of rhetorical theorization of the concept, and the importance of *techne* to assumptions about what is being taught in composition. in underlying writing and the teaching of writing. Thus, rhetorical historiography offers me a critical methodology for examining more adroitly how the 1990s have shaped *techne*’s influence on histories and theories of the discipline.

Furthermore, revisionary histories are often deployed to reinforce “the field’s beliefs instead of critically examining them” (Skinnell, 2015, p. 113), often driven by the field’s urge towards expansion, “searching for people, movements, or methods excluded” (Skinnell, 2015, p. 112) from canonical histories. Recovery, and the original critical perspectives on the rhetorical canons, have been vital to what it might mean to rewrite histories of rhetoric (Ballif, 2013a, p. 1). However, in the continued motions towards broadening and recovery, rhetorical historiographies have tended to promote histories that reinforce the discipline’s ideologies and deflect critique of central assumptions (Skinnell, 2015, p. 118). 

## Data Curation and Data Feminism


**Data Curation and Data Feminism**

Data curation, a carefully considered process of selecting, processing, analyzing, and presenting data, can offer a bridge between the histories of *techne* from the 1990s, the published disciplinary record, field and disciplinary databases, and the perspective that a rhetorical historiography of *techne* in Rhetoric and Composition brings into focus. Through data curation, I explore the diffuse and embedded nature of *techne* in multiple contexts and utilize data feminism in crafting a distributed history of *techne*. Data feminism helps to bring into focus the “matrix of domination” (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020) which infuses disciplinary data and uptakes of *techne*. Data feminism offers a set of seven principles for intersectional feminist thought and approaches to data science and data work: examine power, challenge power, elevate emotion and embodiment, rethink binaries and hierarchies, embrace pluralism, consider context, and make labor visible (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020).  With the principles of examine power, embracing pluralism, and considering context as bridging rhetorical historiography with data feminism. For D’Ignazio and Klein, data feminism approaches how “differentials of power can be challenged and changed using data” (2020), and data curation is developed from this vantage point to offer a critical perspective for the use of data to ask questions within rhetoric and composition without losing sight of people. 

The first principle of data feminism is to examine power. The principle asks that we examine how power is embedded in our data, databases, and publications so that we don’t even see it (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). At the core of my tracing of *techne* lies questions about what gets published, when, and by whom. Ultimately, peer-reviewed publication is an academic currency that has real implications for lived experiences and represents particular power arrangements within the discipline and provides a venue from which these power relations can change. This principle also situated data curation as a rhetorical process that asks how disciplinary histories have been constructed and what the power relationships are of that construction. 

The second principle of embracing pluralism “insists that the most complete knowledge comes from synthesizing multiple perspectives” (D’Iganzio & Klein, 2020). It means attending to the many different voices, what is highlighted, and what is occluded in data, including individuals and their narratives which may be subsumed under data or reduced to points on a graph. This principle has structured an approach that aims to tack in and out (Gold, 2012; Royster, 2012) between data and feminist retellings. This perspective on data has informs data cleaning and presentation stages, because it requires that we ask what gets lost, illuminated, and complicated in data. As a principle, it also bridges difference between texts as data and texts as experiences. I’ve used this principle to focus on moments of multiplicity and cooperation that exist within this project.

Furthermore, the principle of considering context centers the idea that data are not neutral nor objective, but that they are the results of unequal social relationships (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). This has been salient in examining the history of the 1990s, a decade in rhetoric and composition that so often asked and sought questions about the unequal nature of social relations and how that inequity might be addressed through composition. The principle helps to rhetorically contextualize the questions to consider the social, cultural, historical, institutional, and material conditions of the corpus (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). This principle has structured my approaches to the collection of data to make up the corpus, including considerations for the collection, cleaning, storing, and documenting data. The principle also provides an imperative for always considering the context of data, and this context has been important for situating analysis and visualizations within the complex set of contexts out of which data is situated. Further, this principle provides a framework for approaching data from a rhetorical perspective, and data curation helps to map the insights from data feminism to the practices of rhetoric and composition.

Underlying this project is an attention to data feminism and feminist rhetorical historiography. This project assumes that there are rhetorical actions that can be recovered through the uses of data, and that data can be used as a valuable historiographic intervention. This project also assumes that any recovery is a partial perspective. There is a careful feminist attention to power, language, and data within this project that aims to interrogate power within the scholarly record and within the data tools used to trace it. There is a critical attention, or at least an attempt at a critical attention, in this project towards using data science methods with care in order to view differently. Applying data feminism in rhetorical historiography has informed the structure of methods and data collection and approaches to the relationships that analysis and visualizations helped to reveal. The principles of data feminism, especially the three just highlighted for their importance to the structure of this project, provide a means to rhetorically situate my data and analysis.

## Data Curation

My employment of data curation relies on several principles of data feminism, a term partially coined by Catherine D’Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein, reminds us “that before there are data, there are people—people who offer up their experience to be counted and analyzed, people who perform that counting and analysis, people who visualize the data and promote the findings of any particular project, and people who use the product in the end” (2020). By approaching data with rhetorical attitudes that resist closure and historical erasing (Sullivan & Graban, 2011, p. 10), D’Ignazio and Klein present a set of seven data principles for a feminist data ethic: examine power, challenge, power, elevate emotion and embodiment, rethink binaries and hierarchies, embrace pluralism, consider context, and make labor visible (2020). As a feminist practice, data curation focuses on differences and gaps and allows for a revisiting of prior views for an “understanding of how groups of rare artifacts or texts could have functioned together more broadly” (Sullivan & Graban, 2011, p. 7). A feminist data ethics necessitates a data curation which provides an understanding that accounts for “influences of difference on knowledge-production” (Risam, 2015). By examining the published record, generating distant reading insights, exploring citational networks, and curating visualization of the data, a more complete and nuanced history of *techne* emerges. 

Data curation builds from the following assumptions: that textual data analysis can be used to trace an absence of explicit discussion, that this tracing is a partial perspective, that tracing an intersection must account for difference, and that a re-seeing through curation and visualization can be used to make arguments about the current state of the discipline. As data are circulated through technological, political, and social systems (Johnson-Eilola, 1997, p. 288), they become transformed into information, losing connections to their original context in exchange for ease of transportation (Hayles, 1999, pp. 50-57). Data curation, with a careful attention to the ways in which topics have come together and faded, can be an important act of recovery. A feminist approach brings attention to the ways in which data is wielded within information flows (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). By approaching data curation as an embodied historiographic process (Graban & Sullivan, 2018)—telling a story of the field’s communities through selected, visualized, and presented data and discourses (Risam, 2015)—this methodology reconnects information with bodies, data with discourse, and historicity with disciplinarity. 

If we seek distributed histories of *techne* rather than straightforward ones, and if we seek a deeper understanding of how theories of invention have or have not been subsumed under our disciplinary record, then rhetoric and composition’s historiographies will benefit from data-driven approaches that look beyond bibliometrics[^fn1] towards a performance of history (Graban, 2018) and for a citational politics[^fn2] moves from marking citational frequency (Brooke, 2012; Hesse, 2019) and towards better encapsulating a range of scholarly pursuits and engagements over time. By considering the tracing of bibliographic data and citational politics (Brooke, 2012; Mueller, 2018) as a way of directing attention to disciplinary objects within rhetoric and composition, then crafting alternative narratives through digital tools may allow for the visualization of the impacts of citational practices on lived histories and experiences, while moving beyond “historiographies that merely digitize analogue desires” or “remain stuck in a notion of recovery that privileges cumulative advantage” (Graban, 2018, p. 107). Building on available histories in rhetoric and composition—those focusing on writing in colleges (Berlin, 1984), theoretical histories (Hawk, 2007; Mueller, 2017; Pender, 2011), invention (Young, 1976), pedagogy (Fulkerson, 2005) and first-year composition (Skinnell, 2016)—this project proposes a history that offers a view of the discipline beyond singular or deterministic narratives and thus complicates history-telling by re-examining current approaches towards *techne*. 

## Distant Reading - Quantitative Computational Analysis


In a pair of 2023 articles in *Computers and Composition*, John Gallagher and his co-researchers present data-driven approaches to illustrate changes in disciplinary journals through changes in citations and lexical density, and through using computational analysis for emergent key terms (Gallagher et al., 2023). In their discussion of results they note the unique position in writing studies that work with quantitative analysis paired with qualitative analysis to provide explanations of trends seen within data. In their conclusions, they note a “methodological scalar derangement” (Gallagher et al., 2023, p. 16) in writing studies that tends towards the small scale interpretation and critique. They suggest that scale, computation, and big data analysis offer different methodologies that disrupt the habits of readers and push towards intersecting questions and analysis when writing with and for computers, and using big data to measure and track at scale. Furthermore, in a 2017 article in *Digital Humanities Quarterly*, Janichen et. al suggest combining topic models with data visualizations allows for inquiry into large textual data sets and lets researchers to see and discover relationships between discrete documents and within large corpora through visual analysis (Jähnichen et al., 2017).

For Krista Kennedy and Seth Long, data visualization offers new ways of examining the material traces of concepts such as *techne* within scholarship which “can benefit from large textual data sets” through its ability to develop a “more complex view that is simply not possible to attain at a smaller scale” (Kennedy & Long, 2015, p. 142). They are not alone in presuming that data and data visualization can provide rhetoric and composition with a unique methodology for viewing discursive, symbolic, and social actions through “engaging materials worlds” in their rhetorical production (Gruber, 2015, p. 298). At the same time, D’Ignazio and Klein point out the flattening effects of data “because of the values and bias built into data-driven systems” (2020), and a need to account for the power represented within data. Without diminishing the importance of the potentially flattening effects of data visualizations, I look to digital resources to suggest one way of reframing the objects of historical study. D’Ignazio and Klein argue that data feminism asks that we interrogate the conditions of data and data set curation, as well as the people and conditions of their production and to account for the entire lifecycle from gathering to presenting data (2020). In their conclusion, they turn to an expansive conception of data science that collectively interrogates power and works towards justice and change through a focus on individuals and groups (2020). They further illustrate how data can be used to feature voices and to illuminate the unseen within networks of people, data, and power. Data feminism provides me with a foundation for data curation that connects and utilizes data visualization and analysis as a critical and rhetorical stance that helps to trace absences and presences within the disciplinary network represented within the corpus. I use the network metaphor not to claim a truthful presentation of how *techne* has been discussed, but rather to draw attention to where the gaps have been in those discussions, and to give presences to the conversations that have been absent. 

# Methods

I used two primary methods for the investigative data analysis in this project, topic modeling and data visualization. I then paired this investigate data analysis with more the traditional method of critically reading identified articles to examine their historiographic impact. ## **Topic Modeling**

My chosen method conducting investigative data analysis is topic modeling. Topic modeling is a form of natural language processing that statistically calculates the relevance of topics within a corpus of textual data. Through a combination of computational power and statistical analysis, a topic model generates a list of highly plausible topics that can be inferred and read in relationship to a corpus as a form of distant reading in which topics emerge from texts in response to a subjective reading of the generated lists of words. Table 1 provides a selection of topics from the corpus identified as aligning with a compound definition of *techne*. Each topic has a weight value indicating how strongly each bundle of words may be found across the corpus. For example, if one word is found within a document, a grouping with a stronger weight will tend to also include the remaining words. There is then the list of words. And finally is my subjective reading of the topic.

Topic modeling is potentially fruitful for rhetorical study as the topics themselves hold no innate meaning, they are just a jumble of words that tend to be found together, but can be read to infer topics and relationships. By examining Table 1 we can see how a subjective reading of the topics could align with one of Pender’s five composite definitions of *techne.* 

But topic modeling is not without its cautions because it inherently separates the text from its context and offers a view of the construction of the corpus that could be seen as totalizing. Benjamin M. Schmidt, in a 2012 special issue of *The Journal of the Digital Humanities* on topic modeling, offers a rich discussion of the affordances and limitations of topic modeling for humanistic research. By focusing on the shortcomings (along with the potential) he notes that “whatever uses humanists find for topic models, in the end they must integrate the models with a close understanding of the constituent words; and only by returning to describe changes in words can they create meaning.” While topic modeling is not a perfect representation of keyword relationships—because it cannot discern the meaning or usage of the terms, only their frequency within documents alongside the other words in the same topic—using this method has enabled me to infer topical relationships around *techne* based on the likelihood or rarity of particular words occurring together in various combinations across this corpus.

## Data Visualization

I trace impressions of *techne* through the subjective reading of topic models that align with at least one of Pender’s 5 composition definitions of *techne*. I then took the statistical weights on the identified topics and charted them per year using Pandas and Bokeh into the following visualization of the mean weights of the topics identified per year \[Figure 2\]. By hovering over dots on the visualization, the topic mean weight along with the topic number can be seen. Selected topics can be excluded from the visualization by clicking on them in the key. Additionally, the figure can be zoomed in or out to see the trends at different scales.

I want to first discuss reading of the chart. This visualization includes all of the identified topics, and includes all topics with a mean weight of at least .05 for that year. While it is visually busy, there are a few trends that can be noticed. First, the peaks in the visualization represent topics that have a high mean weight. Given the nature of topic modeling, these high mean data points are likely to represent one or a small group of documents with strong correlations with the topic itself. The second trend is the large selection of the visualization devoted to topics with relatively steady means between .1 and .2. These largely horizontal lines with small modulations up and down represent topics that can be found at a lower overall mean but significantly across many more articles. These tend to stay within a relative bound as they can be found more widely, but with perhaps less significance within a particular or smaller selection of articles. While these topics are likely correlated with a larger selection of documents, it is also likely that some subset of the documents has a higher mean weight of those particular topics. However, given the greater distribution of the topic across the documents, the mean is lower. This generates additional areas for further investigative analysis.

When reading the interactive visualization, there are a couple of things to keep in mind. 

1. Weights for the topics are how strongly associated the words within the topic are to each other within the documents of the corpus. So, when modeled using the LDA algorithm, a measure of how likely it is to find that group of words together across the corpus and then within a document is produced.
2. For documents weight is a measure of how much each topic can be found within the document.
3. For the years - I’m looking at the mean weights of the topics per year, or a measure of how much each topic comprises documents for that year. 

As a reminder, topic modeling makes the assumption that words make up topics, and topics make up documents. So, topic modeling works by first figuring out words that are likely to appear together across the whole corpus and assumes those are topics. It then figures out the composition of topics within documents, so what topics are likely to be found in each document and how likely they are to be found there.

## Limitations

There are a number of limitations to this approach to tracing *techne*. First, topic modeling is an imprecise measure with tangible limits. First, you can use the same tools to re-run the model and come up with different topics. Second, this method depends on a level of disciplinary expertise to derive meaning from them topics themselves. Additionally, the produced visualizations are complex and busy, with a potential for significant results to get lost in the noise. Furthermore, even with the tacking in approach to identify and read specific documents, this approach to a corpus is a flattening one that reduces the relationships within the corpus to those derived from the modeling. Also, given the disciplinary context of the corpus, many topics are ones that would be expected from Rhetoric and Composition. Lastly, given the corpus and the limits of topic modeling, many of the topics include metadata references such as journal titles and the names of edited collections.

This tracing opens up a reconsideration of process, especially as it is related to *techne* in the current moment. What the three historiographic impulses demonstrate is that rhetoric and composition has pursued *techne* and its history to provide institutional legitimacy, to argue for certain pedagogies, to account for assumptions, and to recover voices that have been left out of the tradition. However, these impulses and the assumptions underlying them are often left implicit within broader disciplinary contexts surrounding *techne* and are embedded in research tools, databases, and processes. Histories offer a narrative and a legitimacy of a past, and seeking history argues for the value of the present and for future interventions and actions. However, the explicit discussions of the history of *techne* have been lacking, especially when given the context of the 1990s and the social turn.

Through modeling and tracing the corpus, I found *techne* throughout the scholarship of the 1990s though it wasn’t explicitly mentioned outside of specific recoveries of the topic in the history of rhetoric. This absence of *techne* would show up as “impressions” of *techne* in the topic models that I interpreted as a place where it’s not quite there but it could be read as aligning with a composite definition. These impressions of *techne* are embedded within other topics, such as creativity, social critique, and technology, and within documents with titles that would not indicate inclusions of a discussion regarding *techne*. Tracing impressions of *techne* where they appear alongside explicit mentions of invention paints a nuanced picture of the history of *techne* within topics explicitly aligned towards issues of rhetorical and technological invention. The topics identified have a number of readings concerned with the practical application of rhetoric suggesting a rich variety of scholarship that can be drawn upon when considering *techne* and invention. There are also trends in these impressions towards the use of particular resources, such as handbooks, when considering *techne* and the reconsidering of Ancient Greek and Roman rhetorical theory and practice. With these impressions there are three main findings. First, that these impressions largely support existing field histories. Second, histories of *techne* are carried out for two historiographic impulses in authorization and recovery. Third, that looking at impressions rather than direct theorization enriches rhetorical historiographic work.

First, this project supports the narrative of the 1990s as being primarily concerned with the issues of the social turn and postmodern critique, especially within dominant journals. Even when speciality concerns were brought up in more niche journals such as *JAEPL* and *Kairos*, they were framed within discussions of subjectivity, ideology, and social critique. Second, this tracings supports disciplinary histories such as Richard Fulkerson’s that point to continued professionalization and specialization of the field, with impressions of *techne* backing assumptions about the value of teaching and researching writing in particular contexts. However, *techne* was found as an impression within these professionalizing conversations and not as a primary area of historical focus. The continued specialization supports Lauer’s argument that invention scattered into a “diaspora of composition areas,” (2002, p. 2) where discussions of invention were contained within professionalizing focuses. The tracing of impressions of *techne* within the scholarship of the decade helped situate its obscured but important placement, and that it is historicized alongside other concerns. 

Second, my study also suggests that histories of *techne* and invention have been largely undertaken for two historiographic impulses: authorization and recovery. First, as drawn from Ryan Skinnell, authorizing histories of *techne* framed an action, pedagogy, or epistemology within a tradition typically as a broadening narrative in the history rhetoric and composition, and to authorize some approach within that longer tradition (2015, p. 112). Authorizing histories of *techne* were most frequently found alongside discussions of invention and pedagogy and tended to feature topics that discussed the application of particular rhetorical theory and theorists. 

For example, histories of authorization of *techne*, whether explicit or implied, provide a framework for contextualizing and historicizing a certain re-readings of historical figures and canonical texts for a basis of current action, evident in topics relating to Burke, Plato, Aristotle, and Habermas. In authorizing *techne*, documents such as “A Historicist Recontextualization of the Enthymeme” by Carol Poster re-situates uptakes of enthymemes and sophistic rhetoric for contemporary concerns. This, along with other authorizing narratives, connected theories and pedagogies to an intellectual history, building disciplinary legitimacy.

The second historiographic impulse of recovery ultimately has similar goals to those of authorization but, as Michelle Bailiff argues, they work towards an expansion of what counts for the discipline and as a history of rhetoric (2012, p. 1). These histories of recovery included topics that aligned with particular traditions and figures, and tend to feature the redefining of theory through the recovery of a figure or tradition, often with broader rhetorical goals including expanding feminist rhetorics and the reclaiming the sophists. Histories of recovery of *techne* tended towards topics where a specific overlooked rhetor, text, or tradition is brought forward into contemporary scholarship to broaden what counts as history and what should be included within rhetoric. A salient example of this type of history is “Friendly Dress: A Disciplined Use” by Carol Mattingly which focuses on how female Quaker speakers used reporting on their appearance in newspapers as an intentional rhetorical strategy. This article is itself situated within a number of other articles recovering a variety of figures from, and in turning towards overlooked rhetorical traditions and to frame current practices. 

When modeling the corpus, both of these historiographies tended operate at two different levels, authorizing at a macro level which tends to examine major movements in order to make authorizing claims about the discipline as a whole, and recovery at a micro level that tends to examine the local and specific, looking to make smaller recoveries in support of marginalized traditions or an expansion of a local context. An examination of history through a trace of *techne* illustrates how historiographic impulses and scales operate for different purposes in the discipline and are often employed to address different question about the nature of invention and *techne*. While diffuse and obscured, there is an abundance of discussions around *techne* and invention that is often embedded within other conversations.

In conclusion, these two historiographies of authorization and recovery are contextualized within a discipline focused on social construction and critique in the 1990s. As Geoffrey Sirc laments, the 1990s were a decade of “conservative professionalism” (2002, p. 32) that focused on using the potentials of postmodernism and linguistic play to better critique others and to secure a spot within the academy instead of an opening up of possibilities after process in *techne*. With the importation of critical theory into rhetoric and composition, invention appeared to become buried under these critical perspectives which John Muckelbauer argues bridges postmodern and social concerns between creation and discovery (Muckelbauer 2008, p. 13). Tracing *techne* suggests approaching the diffusion of interests brought about by professionalization and specialization as an opportunity to theorize the distributed impacts and histories of *techne*, and to reevaluate the value that has been placed on invention within these contexts. This project considers a *techne* that is more enriched for uptake in disciplinary conversations, and the results suggest enriching our current histories. Both authorizing and recovering narratives are important for consideration within the histories of the discipline, and we might recoup histories of invention through tracing at both micro and macro scales.
