Methodology

Data Curation

My employment of data curation relies on several principles of data feminism, a term partially coined by Catherine D’Ignazio and Lauren F. Klein, reminds us “that before there are data, there are people—people who offer up their experience to be counted and analyzed, people who perform that counting and analysis, people who visualize the data and promote the findings of any particular project, and people who use the product in the end” (2020). By approaching data with rhetorical attitudes that resist closure and historical erasing (Sullivan & Graban, 2011, p. 10), D’Ignazio and Klein present a set of seven data principles for a feminist data ethic: examine power, challenge, power, elevate emotion and embodiment, rethink binaries and hierarchies, embrace pluralism, consider context, and make labor visible (2020). As a feminist practice, data curation focuses on differences and gaps and allows for a revisiting of prior views for an “understanding of how groups of rare artifacts or texts could have functioned together more broadly” (Sullivan & Graban, 2011, p. 7). A feminist data ethics necessitates a data curation which provides an understanding that accounts for “influences of difference on knowledge-production” (Risam, 2015). By examining the published record, generating distant reading insights, exploring citational networks, and curating visualization of the data, a more complete and nuanced history of techne emerges.

Data curation builds from the following assumptions: that textual data analysis can be used to trace an absence of explicit discussion, that this tracing is a partial perspective, that tracing an intersection must account for difference, and that a re-seeing through curation and visualization can be used to make arguments about the current state of the discipline. As data are circulated through technological, political, and social systems (Johnson-Eilola, 1997, p. 288), they become transformed into information, losing connections to their original context in exchange for ease of transportation (Hayles, 1999, pp. 50-57). Data curation, with a careful attention to the ways in which topics have come together and faded, can be an important act of recovery. A feminist approach brings attention to the ways in which data is wielded within information flows (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). By approaching data curation as an embodied historiographic process (Graban & Sullivan, 2018)—telling a story of the field’s communities through selected, visualized, and presented data and discourses (Risam, 2015)—this methodology reconnects information with bodies, data with discourse, and historicity with disciplinarity.

If we seek distributed histories of techne rather than straightforward ones, and if we seek a deeper understanding of how theories of invention have or have not been subsumed under our disciplinary record, then rhetoric and composition’s historiographies will benefit from data-driven approaches that look beyond bibliometrics[1] towards a performance of history (Graban, 2018) and for a citational politics[2] moves from marking citational frequency (Brooke, 2012; Hesse, 2019) and towards better encapsulating a range of scholarly pursuits and engagements over time. By considering the tracing of bibliographic data and citational politics (Brooke, 2012; Mueller, 2018) as a way of directing attention to disciplinary objects within rhetoric and composition, then crafting alternative narratives through digital tools may allow for the visualization of the impacts of citational practices on lived histories and experiences, while moving beyond “historiographies that merely digitize analogue desires” or “remain stuck in a notion of recovery that privileges cumulative advantage” (Graban, 2018, p. 107). Building on available histories in rhetoric and composition—those focusing on writing in colleges (Berlin, 1984), theoretical histories (Hawk, 2007; Mueller, 2017; Pender, 2011), invention (Young, 1976), pedagogy (Fulkerson, 2005) and first-year composition (Skinnell, 2016)—this project proposes a history that offers a view of the discipline beyond singular or deterministic narratives and thus complicates history-telling by re-examining current approaches towards techne.

Distant Reading - Quantitative Computational Analysis

In a pair of 2023 articles in Computers and Composition, John Gallagher and his co-researchers present data-driven approaches to illustrate changes in disciplinary journals through changes in citations and lexical density, and through using computational analysis for emergent key terms (Gallagher et al., 2023). In their discussion of results they note the unique position in writing studies that work with quantitative analysis paired with qualitative analysis to provide explanations of trends seen within data. In their conclusions, they note a “methodological scalar derangement” (Gallagher et al., 2023, p. 16) in writing studies that tends towards the small scale interpretation and critique. They suggest that scale, computation, and big data analysis offer different methodologies that disrupt the habits of readers and push towards intersecting questions and analysis when writing with and for computers, and using big data to measure and track at scale. Furthermore, in a 2017 article in Digital Humanities Quarterly, Janichen et. al suggest combining topic models with data visualizations allows for inquiry into large textual data sets and lets researchers to see and discover relationships between discrete documents and within large corpora through visual analysis (Jähnichen et al., 2017).

For Krista Kennedy and Seth Long, data visualization offers new ways of examining the material traces of concepts such as techne within scholarship which “can benefit from large textual data sets” through its ability to develop a “more complex view that is simply not possible to attain at a smaller scale” (Kennedy & Long, 2015, p. 142). They are not alone in presuming that data and data visualization can provide rhetoric and composition with a unique methodology for viewing discursive, symbolic, and social actions through “engaging materials worlds” in their rhetorical production (Gruber, 2015, p. 298). At the same time, D’Ignazio and Klein point out the flattening effects of data “because of the values and bias built into data-driven systems” (2020), and a need to account for the power represented within data. Without diminishing the importance of the potentially flattening effects of data visualizations, I look to digital resources to suggest one way of reframing the objects of historical study. D’Ignazio and Klein argue that data feminism asks that we interrogate the conditions of data and data set curation, as well as the people and conditions of their production and to account for the entire lifecycle from gathering to presenting data (2020). In their conclusion, they turn to an expansive conception of data science that collectively interrogates power and works towards justice and change through a focus on individuals and groups (2020). They further illustrate how data can be used to feature voices and to illuminate the unseen within networks of people, data, and power. Data feminism provides me with a foundation for data curation that connects and utilizes data visualization and analysis as a critical and rhetorical stance that helps to trace absences and presences within the disciplinary network represented within the corpus. I use the network metaphor not to claim a truthful presentation of how techne has been discussed, but rather to draw attention to where the gaps have been in those discussions, and to give presences to the conversations that have been absent.
