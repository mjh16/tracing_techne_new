## The Trouble with Tracing _Techne_

The 2018 publication of *Composition, Rhetoric, and Disciplinarity* marks a recently renewed attempt to identify and qualify the contemporary social moment in Rhetoric and Composition (Malenczyk et al.). Part of the commonality that binds Rhetoric and Composition together in our contemporary moment, as Malenczyk et al. describe it, is a devotion to pedagogy and an orientation toward service defined by inquiry into manifestations of language in this thing we call writing that make it look different from other disciplines (2018, pp. 4-5). In that publication, what Malenczyk et al call a “disciplinary” turn  invites a critical examination of the field’s history. As a collection, *Composition, Rhetoric, and Disciplinarity* draws attention to the assumptions and work of the field of Rhetoric and Composition and, in doing so, asks us to re-examine the foundations of what it means to study writing.

## Why *techne,* why now?

*Techne* is an important assumptions for writing, rhetoric, and the teaching of writing given its basis in the art and craft of rhetoric. There is a need for considering that there is something that is teachable, learnable, and masterable with rhetoric as a disciplinary concern. A *techne,* a skill or ability, is needed in order to act rhetorically. Additionally, there is a sense of what might be necessary to consider various levels of attainment or mastery, but not a clear sense of how one is considered at those different levels without some clear guidance. *Techne* is assumed and theorized as necessary for a rhetor to exist and to make change.  However, as Kelly Pender (2011), Byron Hawk (2007), and Jacqueline Rhodes and Jonathon Alexander (2015) continue to demonstrate, *techne* is a slippery concept. Some of the difficulty in considering what *techne* is and what its role in current theory and pedagogy is the multiplicity of definitions, traditions, and assumptions. Additionally, there are disagreements on the value and role of *techne* itself that derive from these assumptions. 

Another issue, as most succinctly, presented by Ulmer (1994), is that *techne* often becomes conflated with invention. It’s more than just invention, a where/how ideas and discourse emerge, but the interwoven notions of invention, creativity, design, production, and teaching where *techne* resides. *Techne* is a valuable concept for understanding the epistemologies of the discipline of Rhetoric and Composition because of its ability to connect invention, language, and people “with the process of making” (Pender, 2011, p. 4) and has implications for how we understand where writing comes from and how we make epistemological claims about writing. Much as Janice M. Lauer point to invention being scattered to a number of places in the 1990s (2002) so to does *techne*. 	Part of the ambiguity and complexity of *techne*, I would argue, is its positioning between invention and generous conceptions of creativity and technology. Furthermore, *techne* also intersects with issues of power, privilege, literacy, and access, and has deep implications for who is able to speak/write, but also if/how others might listen and be moved. Further, notions of *techne* are further complicated by the historiography of the concept. How history is made is a rhetorical process, and has deep implications for how disciplines develop (Baliff,  2013; Graban & Sullivan, 2018). Historiography has influenced disciplinary assumptions, histories, theories, practices, and pedagogies. As important as history is, historiography is equally as important, especially, I would argue, for disciplines.

*Techne* seems especially important for the development of the discipline of Rhetoric and Composition in the 1990s - and for discussions and arguments of disciplinarity for Rhetoric and Composition then and now—so it needs to be contended with. In contending with the historiography of *techne* in/from the 1990s, this study is guided by two overarching questions:

* Where is *techne* in 1990s scholarship?
* What are the implications of the uptake of *techne* in the 1990s?

However, there is little direct theorization in the 1990s of *techne*. When starting this project, I searched for “techne” in *CompPile*, a common disciplinary repository and search tool, bounded to the years 1990-1999, and came up with 9 results including two doctoral thesis and an article in German. Perhaps *techne* is a casualty of the post-process turn in the field. Maybe as issues of process, and especially cognition were turned aside for important cultural and social critiques, issues of *techne* were subsumed in other conversation. Maybe they were overlooked entirely. However, *techne* does get picked up in the early 2000s and onward with work by Atwill, Lauer, and Sirc with in relationship to theories of invention and how they figure into rhetorical pedagogy with digital technology. 

## **The 1990s**

I find the 1990s interesting for its lasting influence on questions of disciplinarity and rhetorical conceptions of writing in curriculums and pedagogy. Looking back at the 1990s with its influence of another major turn, the so-called “social” turn of the 1980s (Trimbur, 1994, p. 109), and the importing of computerized technology into the teaching of writing, invites a recouping of *techne* for use in our current moment. I was particularly interested in that ambiguous space where writing gets started, those inventive and creative moments. So I set out on a historiographic project that was interested in the interactions of invention, creativity, and text-technologies and the influence of 1990s scholarship on current theories and practices. In undergoing initial searches in academic databases such as *CompPile*, JSTOR, and Ebscohost. What I found was the same issues that Janet M. Atwil and Janice M. Lauer noted in their chapters in *Perspectives on Rhetorical Invention*. What I found was *techne*, and that in the 1990s, that just like theories of invention *techne* was everywhere and nowhere.

Invention is a concept that is central to rhetorical theory and practice and plays a formative role in the composition pedagogies (Lauer, 2004, p. 1). Invention was central to Richard Young’s 1978 conception of a paradigm of process around which to construct the teaching and research of composition. More specifically, the naming of a disciplinary turn invites us to chart the multiple trajectories and definitions of invention, given the prevalence and diffusion of invention scholarship during historiographic and professionalizing contexts of the 1990s which may bring other disciplinary tensions into focus. It also invites retheorizing invention for creative pedagogies which bring together the personal, where *techne* connects processes of invention to processes of creating discourse. This moment of critical reflection, in turn, invites an examination of historiographic assumptions around *techne*, interrogating the published record for how that history has been formed and circulated for current pedagogies and theories.
